What is reaction time?

Reaction time is the gap between your senses picking up a signal — a colour change on screen, a sound — and your body actually responding, such as pressing a key. The chain runs from the visual signal reaching your visual cortex, to your brain making a decision and issuing a command, to that command travelling down to the muscles in your hand. The whole path usually takes only a few hundred milliseconds, but it varies from person to person and even from moment to moment.

What affects your reaction time

Plenty of things move the number: age (young adults are typically quicker than children and older adults), sleep quality, how focused you are, distractions, stimulants such as caffeine, and athletic training. Even your screen's refresh rate and mouse or touch latency can account for tens of milliseconds. Professionals who depend on split-second responses — esports players, fighters, racing drivers — often train their simple reaction time down below 200 ms.

How the test works

The box starts blue. Click it and it turns red, which means the waiting phase has begun — don't click during this part. After a random delay of 1.5 to 5 seconds it turns green, and the moment you see green you click, which records your reaction time in milliseconds. Clicking while the box is still red counts as a false start and you have to begin again. That rule exists on purpose, to check that you're genuinely reacting to the signal rather than guessing on a fixed rhythm.

How to read your score

Simple visual reaction time for most adults sits somewhere between 200 and 350 ms, with roughly 250 ms as a common midpoint. It's worth running several rounds and looking at your average rather than your single best: any one attempt carries a slice of luck, whether your finger happened to be poised already or your attention drifted for a beat.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does this test actually measure?
It measures your simple reaction time — the interval between the signal appearing on screen (turning green) and your click, in milliseconds (1000 ms = 1 second). It reflects how quickly the visual signal reaches your brain and then travels to your finger muscles, not intelligence or any broader ability.
What is an average reaction time?
Most adults land around 250 ms for simple visual reaction time, with roughly 200–350 ms all counting as normal. Esports players and professional athletes often hold steady under 200 ms after extensive training, but there's no need to judge yourself harshly against that — individual variation is completely expected.
Why was I slower this time than last time?
Reaction time swings with your current state, which is entirely normal. Common causes include poor sleep, alcohol, screen fatigue after long sessions, distraction (scrolling or talking at the same time), and differences in device, mouse or touch latency. A 20–50 ms swing between attempts is ordinary, so run several rounds and read the average instead.
Does caffeine really speed up reaction time?
There is research supporting the idea that a moderate dose — about one cup of coffee — briefly raises alertness and shaves a little off reaction time. The effect varies between individuals, though, and too much caffeine can cause shakiness and anxiety that hurt fine motor stability. Treating coffee as a scoring strategy isn't a good habit.
Is this test accurate enough for medical or professional use?
The timing runs in your browser, so device performance, screen refresh rate and any network latency all introduce some error compared with laboratory-grade reaction time equipment. It's well suited to casual testing and comparing with friends, but it shouldn't be used for medical diagnosis, driving licence assessments, professional athlete selection, or any other formal purpose.
